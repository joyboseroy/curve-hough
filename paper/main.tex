\begin{abstract}
We extend the Deep Hough Transform (DHT), which detects semantic lines by aggregating learned features along candidate lines into a two-dimensional parameter space, to higher-dimensional parametric curve families. The extension is hard because the accumulator for a family with $d$ parameters has $O(B^{d})$ cells for $B$ bins per axis. We propose a factorized deep Hough transform, a differentiable feature-space voting cascade that splits the parameter vector into a spatial anchor and a shape. A coarse two-dimensional accumulator votes for anchors, and a dense shape accumulator is built only around the top-$k$ anchors, so memory falls from $O(B^{d})$ to $O(B^{d_1}+kB^{d_2})$. Lines are the degenerate case with no second stage. We also define a curve agreement score that extends the EA line similarity to parametric curves, and a synthetic benchmark of parabolas, circles and ellipses with noise, occlusion and distractors.

We evaluate with thresholds selected on a validation split and the test split scored once, and we give the geometric baselines the same treatment. Under this protocol the choice of Stage-1 pooling over probe shapes matters a great deal: max pooling raises parabola F from $0.51$ to $0.70$ over log-sum-exp (3 seeds). With it, the cascade ties a tuned RANSAC fit on parabolas ($0.706$ vs.\ $0.702$) and circles ($0.743$ vs.\ $0.742$) and is slightly behind it on ellipses ($0.382$ vs.\ $0.410$), while direct regression and query-based heads reach $0.15$ and $0.13$ on parabolas. So the cascade is competitive with classical fitting rather than better than it, and its practical advantage is memory: at $B=32$ a dense $d=5$ accumulator would need about 4\,GB (computed, not instantiated) against a measured 236\,KB for the cascade. We locate the remaining error in Stage 1. Running Stage 2 at the ground-truth anchors gives F $=0.938$ against $0.706$, and curves whose anchors lie within about 8 pixels of each other lose about 0.2 F because their Stage-1 peaks merge. We also report a TuSimple lane-detection stress test, which we treat as a feasibility check rather than a competitive result. Code and the benchmark generator are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The Hough transform \citep{hough1962,duda1972} detects a parametric shape by letting evidence vote for every shape that could explain it. For lines, each edge pixel votes for all lines through it, and peaks in the $(\theta,r)$ space are detections. The Deep Hough Transform (DHT) \citep{zhao2021deep} moves this into a network: instead of voting edge pixels it averages learned CNN features along every candidate line, finds peaks in the parameter space with a small convolutional head, and trains everything end to end.

Classical Hough theory has been extended to circles, ellipses and algebraic curves \citep{ballard1981,torrente2014,massone2015}, but the feature-space formulation has stayed with lines \citep{zhao2021deep,lin2020houghpriors,houglanenet2023}. The reason is cost. A line has two parameters, so its accumulator is a small map. A vertex-form parabola or a circle has three, an ellipse five, and a dense accumulator with $B$ bins per axis has $B^{d}$ cells, each holding a $C$-channel feature. Deep curve detectors therefore moved to direct parameter regression \citep{polylanenet2020,lstr2021,feng2022bezier}, which gives up the explicit evidence surface that voting provides.

Our approach follows the multi-stage ellipse detectors of the classical literature \citep{yuen1989ellipse}: find where the curve is first, then what shape it has. We write the parameters as an anchor and a shape. Stage 1 votes over anchors only, pooling over a small set of probe shapes. Stage 2 votes over shape only, and only around the $k$ strongest anchors. The cost of the accumulators drops from $O(B^{d})$ to $O(B^{d_1}+kB^{d_2})$.

The detector is family-conditional: the target family and its parameterization are specified in advance, as in classical Hough detection. The question we ask is whether DHT-style feature-space voting can be made tractable once a family of dimension $d>2$ is given. It is not an attempt at open-world shape discovery.

Parameter-space factorization itself is not new, since multi-stage Hough methods have a long history. What we contribute is its formulation as a differentiable deep-feature voting cascade, in which learned image features are aggregated over conditional curve hypotheses and trained through peak losses in every accumulator. We use validation-selected thresholds throughout and report an ablation of the Stage-1 pooling operator, which turns out to matter a great deal.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Method.} A factorized deep Hough transform for curve families with a spatial anchor, with lines as the degenerate case (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and a curve-EA similarity that extends the EA line score (Section~\ref{sec:metric}).
  \item \textbf{Protocol.} A benchmark generator and an evaluation protocol with validation-selected thresholds, matched baselines that include tuned RANSAC for parabolas, circles and ellipses, and ablations (Sections~\ref{sec:protocol} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis.} A decomposition of the cascade's error into Stage 1 and Stage 2 using an oracle-anchor ceiling, top-$k$ anchor recall and an anchor-separation sweep (Section~\ref{sec:stage1}). Stage 1 is the bottleneck, and nearby anchors are a quantified failure region.
  \item \textbf{Scope of the claim.} At $d=3$ the cascade matches a tuned RANSAC fit and at $d=5$ it is somewhat behind. What it adds is differentiable feature-space voting with memory that grows with the shape dimension only (Section~\ref{sec:memory}). We do not claim better accuracy than classical fitting.
  \item \textbf{Real-image stress test.} A TuSimple evaluation with a structural split into near-straight and curved lanes (Section~\ref{sec:real}), reported with its limitations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Classical Hough transforms for curves.}
The Hough transform was introduced for bubble chamber photographs \citep{hough1962} and formalized for lines with the angle-radius parameterization \citep{duda1972}. \citet{ballard1981} generalized voting to arbitrary shapes. Dimensionality is the main obstacle. Randomized \citep{xu1990rht} and probabilistic \citep{kiryati1991} variants subsample votes, adaptive variants refine the grid \citep{illingworth1987}, and multi-stage methods split the parameter space, for example detecting ellipse centers before axes \citep{yuen1989ellipse}. Hough methods for special algebraic curves have been used for anatomical profile extraction in CT \citep{torrente2014,massone2015}. Our cascade is a differentiable, feature-space version of this decomposition. For ellipses, RANSAC with a direct conic fit \citep{fischler1981ransac,fitzgibbon1999ellipse} is a strong classical alternative, and we compare against it.

\paragraph{Deep Hough methods.}
DHT \citep{zhao2021deep} aggregates CNN features along candidate lines into $(\theta,r)$ space, detects peaks with convolutions, and introduces the EA metric and the NKL dataset. \citet{lin2020houghpriors} add trainable Hough line priors inside a CNN. HoughLaneNet \citep{houglanenet2023} applies a hierarchical DHT to lane detection, but the voting is line-based and curves are recovered afterwards by dynamic convolution. Deep Hough voting is also used for 3D object centroids \citep{qi2019votenet}. We are not aware of prior feature-space voting over curve parameter spaces.

\paragraph{Deep curve detection by regression.}
PolyLaneNet \citep{polylanenet2020} regresses polynomial coefficients, LSTR \citep{lstr2021} predicts polynomial lane shapes with a transformer, BezierLaneNet \citep{feng2022bezier} predicts cubic Bezier control points, and BSNet \citep{bsnet2023} uses B-splines. These avoid the accumulator but also give up an explicit evidence surface. Our regression and DETR-style baselines \citep{detr2020} stand in for this family at small scale.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Deep Hough voting}
Let $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$ be encoder features. For a curve family with parameters $\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}$, let $\Omega(\mathbf{p})$ be the pixel set of the curve $C(\mathbf{p})$ clipped to the image. Voting computes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{1}{|\Omega(\mathbf{p})|}\sum_{i\in\Omega(\mathbf{p})}\mathbf{X}(i),
\label{eq:vote}
\end{equation}
which for lines and a quantized $\mathcal{P}$ is the DHT of \citet{zhao2021deep} up to normalization. A small convolutional head on $\mathbf{Y}$ gives detections. With $B$ bins per axis, $\mathbf{Y}$ has $C\cdot B^{d}$ entries.

\subsection{Parameter factorization and scope}
We write $\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s})$ with an \emph{anchor} $\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_1}$ (a spatial point: parabola vertex, circle or ellipse center) and a \emph{shape} $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_2}$ (curvature, radius, axes and orientation). For a vertex-form parabola $y=s(x-a_1)^2+a_2$ we have $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,a_2)$ and $\mathbf{s}=s$. For a circle, $\mathbf{a}$ is the center and $\mathbf{s}=r$. For an ellipse, $\mathbf{a}$ is the center and $\mathbf{s}=(r_x,r_y,\phi)$.

The method assumes that the curve family is known and that it has a spatially meaningful anchor. Both assumptions are shared with classical Hough voting, which also searches a fixed hypothesis space, and both are by design: the accumulator needs a parameterization to index. A family without such an anchor would need a different factorization, and we do not address it.

\paragraph{Lines as the degenerate case.} A line has no anchor separate from its own parameters, so $d_1=d=2$, $d_2=0$, and Stage 2 is empty. The cascade is then single-stage voting over $(\theta,\rho)$, which is the original DHT. Lines use the same encoder, training loop and detection code as the other families, and differ only in whether Stage 2 is built. This lets the real-image experiment in Section~\ref{sec:real} use one cascade for both near-straight and curved lanes.

\subsection{Stage 1: marginal anchor voting}
Stage 1 builds an accumulator over anchors only, pooling over a small probe set $\tilde{\mathcal{S}}=\{\mathbf{s}_1,\dots,\mathbf{s}_m\}$ of shapes:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}_1(\mathbf{a})=\operatorname*{pool}_{\mathbf{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}\;\frac{1}{|\Omega(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s})|}\sum_{i\in\Omega(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s})}\mathbf{X}(i).
\label{eq:stage1}
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{Y}_1$ has $C\cdot B_a^{2}$ entries. A small 2D convolutional head turns it into an anchor map $\mathbf{P}_1$. The pooling operator is a design choice that matters. We compare log-sumexp and max in Section~\ref{sec:pooling}; mean pooling was not evaluated. Max pooling reads as ``some probe shape fits here'', which is the right question for localizing an anchor when the shape is unknown, and it is our default. The probe set has $m=6$ shapes for parabolas and circles, and $m=27$ for ellipses (three values per shape axis).

\subsection{Stage 2: conditional shape voting}
For each of the top-$k$ anchor peaks $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j$ (3$\times$3 local maxima of $\mathbf{P}_1$, ranked by score), Stage 2 builds a dense accumulator over shape only:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}_2^{(j)}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{|\Omega(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j,\mathbf{s})|}\sum_{i\in\Omega(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j,\mathbf{s})}\mathbf{X}(i),\qquad \mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{dense}}.
\label{eq:stage2}
\end{equation}
A convolutional head and a peak selection give the full detection $(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j,\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j)$ with a score. Soft-argmax over the local neighborhood gives sub-bin precision in both stages. Re-voting on a finer grid around each detection, in the manner of coarse-to-fine classical Hough \citep{illingworth1987}, is not implemented, and nothing reported here uses it.

\begin{proposition}[Accumulator size]
With $\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_1}$, $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_2}$, $d_1+d_2=d$ and $B$ bins per axis, the dense accumulator of Eq.~\ref{eq:vote} has $O(B^{d})$ entries, and the cascade has $O(B^{d_1}+kB^{d_2})$ entries, where $k$ is the number of retained anchors.
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof.} Stage 1 accumulates over $\mathbf{a}$ only. The probe set has size $m$ independent of $B$, so Stage 1 holds $B^{d_1}$ cells per channel. Stage 2 is built at $k$ anchors and each holds $B^{d_2}$ cells per channel. $\blacksquare$

The bound is about accumulator memory. It says nothing about accuracy, and the factorization is lossy: Stage 2 only sees anchors that Stage 1 retained. Section~\ref{sec:stage1} measures how much that costs.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Factorized deep Hough cascade (inference)}
\label{alg:cascade}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathbf{X}\gets\text{Encoder}(\text{image})$
\For{each anchor bin $\mathbf{a}$}
  \State $\mathbf{Y}_1(\mathbf{a})\gets\max_{\mathbf{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}\ \text{mean}_{i\in\Omega(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s})}\mathbf{X}(i)$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:stage1}}
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{P}_1\gets\text{ConvHead}_1(\mathbf{Y}_1)$; \quad $\{\hat{\mathbf{a}}_1,\dots,\hat{\mathbf{a}}_k\}\gets\text{TopK-NMS}(\mathbf{P}_1)$
\For{each retained anchor $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j$}
  \For{each shape bin $\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{dense}}$}
    \State $\mathbf{Y}_2^{(j)}(\mathbf{s})\gets\text{mean}_{i\in\Omega(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j,\mathbf{s})}\mathbf{X}(i)$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:stage2}}
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathbf{P}_2^{(j)}\gets\text{ConvHead}_2(\mathbf{Y}_2^{(j)})$; \quad $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j\gets\text{SoftArgmaxPeak}(\mathbf{P}_2^{(j)})$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\{(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_j,\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j,\text{score}_j)\}_{j=1}^{k}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Training}
As in \citet{zhao2021deep}, ground-truth curves are mapped into each accumulator, smoothed with a Gaussian, and each stage is trained with binary cross-entropy. Stage 2 targets are conditioned on the ground-truth anchors (teacher forcing) and on detected anchors within a matching radius. The voting layers are parameter-free gather-and-mean operations, so the encoder and both heads train end to end. The two losses are summed with equal weight. Stage-1 targets use a Gaussian of $\sigma=1.5$ bins and a positive-class weight of 8, and Stage-2 targets use $\sigma=1.0$ bins. All runs use Adam with learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, batch size 8 and 10 epochs. The anchor grid has $B_a=32$ bins per axis and the dense shape grid $B_s=48$ bins, with $C=32$ channels and $k=4$.

\section{The Curve-EA Score}
\label{sec:metric}
The EA score of \citet{zhao2021deep} multiplies an angular and a midpoint distance term, both specific to lines. For curves $c_1,c_2$ we sample $T$ points $\{u^{(1)}_t\},\{u^{(2)}_t\}$ uniformly in arc length inside the image and define a normalized symmetric Chamfer term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_d=1-\min\!\left(1,\frac{\operatorname{CD}(\{u^{(1)}\},\{u^{(2)}\})}{D_{\max}}\right),
\end{equation}
with $D_{\max}$ the image diagonal times a constant, and a tangent agreement term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_\theta=1-\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\frac{\angle\big(\tau_1(u^{(1)}_t),\tau_2(\pi(u^{(1)}_t))\big)}{\pi/2},
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ are unit tangents and $\pi(\cdot)$ is nearest-point correspondence. The score is $\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{S}_d\cdot\mathcal{S}_\theta)^2$ in $[0,1]$. Predictions and ground truth are matched by the Hungarian algorithm and precision, recall and F are averaged over a grid of similarity thresholds, as in \citet{zhao2021deep}. Because the grid includes lenient thresholds, a detection whose anchor is a few pixels off can still count as a match. This matters when reading the anchor-recall numbers below.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Benchmark.} Images are $64\times64$ in all reported results (the generator supports $128\times128$). Each image has one to three curves from one family, drawn with stroke width 2, plus Gaussian pixel noise ($\sigma_n=0.08$ unless varied), up to two random occluding rectangles ($\text{occ\_max}=2$), and up to three distractor line segments. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark_examples} shows examples.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/benchmark_examples.png}
\caption{Benchmark images, clean (top) and corrupted (bottom: noise, occlusion, distractor segments), for each curve family.}
\label{fig:benchmark_examples}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Splits and seeds.} Each run trains on 1{,}000 images and uses a validation split of 200 images and a test split of 200 images, all drawn from the same generator with different seeds. The seed of a run changes model initialization, data order and the three image draws, so the standard deviations we report over seeds include data variance. Differences below about 0.02 F are within noise at this size: two runs of the same configuration and seed gave 0.706 and 0.690.

\paragraph{Threshold selection.} A detection is kept if its score is at least a threshold $\tau$. We sweep $\tau$ over $\{0.05,0.10,\dots,0.95\}$ on the validation split, pick the best, and score the test split once at that value. As a check we also run a test-set sweep and report how much it would have overstated F. Across all runs reported here the gap is between 0.000 and 0.011, so the choice of protocol changes the numbers little.

\paragraph{Baselines.} All baselines use the same images, metric and splits.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{RANSAC} on Sobel edge points: a 3-point minimal solver for parabolas and circles, and a 5-point conic fit with Sampson distance for ellipses \citep{fitzgibbon1999ellipse,hartley2004mvg}. \emph{Tuned} RANSAC keeps candidates whose inlier count passes a threshold chosen on validation, which is the treatment the learned detectors get. \emph{Untuned} RANSAC always keeps the top four candidates.
  \item \emph{Classical dense Hough} on Sobel edges, with a tuned accumulator threshold. It runs at 16 anchor bins and 16 shape bins, coarser than the cascade's 32, so it is handicapped. It is not run for ellipses, where a dense 5D accumulator is infeasible at useful resolution.
  \item \emph{Direct regression}: the same encoder and an MLP head that predicts up to three parameter vectors, trained with a permutation-matched L1 loss.
  \item \emph{DETR-lite queries}: the same encoder and a small DETR-style decoder with learned queries and Hungarian matching.
\end{itemize}
The two learned baselines get the same 1{,}000 training images and 10 epochs as the cascade. They are small models trained with a small budget, so their numbers describe this regime and should not be read as a bound on what regression or query heads can do with more data.

\subsection{Where the cascade loses accuracy}
\label{sec:stage1}
Stage 2 only runs at anchors that Stage 1 retains, so a missed or misplaced anchor cannot be repaired. We measure this in three ways, all for parabolas with max pooling and seed 0.

\paragraph{Oracle-anchor ceiling.} Running Stage 2 at the ground-truth anchors, with Stage 1 removed, gives F $=0.938$, against $0.706$ for the real pipeline. Stage 1 costs 0.232 F, and Stage 2 is not the limiting stage.

This is a strict localization measure and not a bound on recall in the F metric, which accepts detections whose anchor is a few pixels off.

Beyond two anchors the extra candidates are mostly duplicates or near-misses of curves already found, and they lower precision as much as they raise recall. This agrees with the diagnosis above: more retained peaks do not repair a mislocalized or merged anchor, so the fix has to be in Stage 1 itself and not in $k$.

Two runs of the same configuration and seed gave 0.706 (Table~\ref{tab:main}) and 0.690 here, a spread of 0.016 that we have not traced, probably nondeterministic GPU kernels. We therefore treat single-run differences below about 0.02 as noise throughout.

\section{TuSimple Stress Test}
\label{sec:real}
We also ran the cascade on TuSimple \citep{tusimple2017}, a dashcam lane dataset, letterboxed to $128\times128$. This is a feasibility check on real photographs. It is not a competitive lane-detection benchmark, and the results below are weaker in evidence than the synthetic ones above: they come from one run per setting, they predate the validation-threshold protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, and we have not evaluated the published lane detectors or the DHT-based lane method of related work under our metric. Those methods report the official TuSimple accuracy, which our curve-EA F is not comparable to, so we give no side-by-side numbers.

\subsection{A structural split}
TuSimple labels lanes as polylines. We fit each lane to a parabola $x=a(y-y_0)^2+x_0$ by least squares. Of 10{,}889 lanes with at least three labeled points, 3{,}823 (35.1\%) give a numerically stable fit and 7{,}066 (64.9\%) are close to straight, so the vertex-form conversion is unstable. We checked this by bounding the fitted vertex against the image extent, not by a curvature threshold alone, which let unstable fits through. We send near-straight lanes to the line family (no Stage 2) and curved lanes to the parabola family, and train and evaluate the two as separate models. A single detector that handles both regimes in one image is not implemented, and the two-model setup is a limitation.

A second issue appeared when we inspected Stage-1 outputs on real data. A parabola fit can be stable while its vertex lies outside the photographed scene. Our anchor grid covers only the image, so such targets cannot be learned. We drop lanes whose transformed vertex is more than half an image dimension outside the frame. On held-out curved lanes this roughly doubled F relative to training on the unfiltered set, and training on the smaller filtered set did better than training on the larger one.

\section{Discussion: Voting as Geometry-Constrained Attention}
\label{sec:discussion}
Eq.~\ref{eq:vote} is a cross-attention read-out in which hypothesis $\mathbf{p}$ attends to pixel tokens with a fixed attention map: weight $1/|\Omega(\mathbf{p})|$ on the curve's pixels and zero elsewhere. A DETR decoder learns this map. That gives it flexibility and removes the geometric prior. Table~\ref{tab:attention_compare} summarizes the contrast. Our parabola numbers (Table~\ref{tab:main}) are consistent with the prior helping at small scale, but, as noted above, they do not isolate the prior from the training budget.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Family-conditional detection.} The target family is specified at inference time. This is deliberate and matches classical Hough voting, which also needs a parameterization of what it looks for, but it excludes open-world shape discovery and free-form curves. The factorization also needs a spatially meaningful anchor. A family-agnostic system could treat the family as another discrete hypothesis variable or learn a library of hypothesis spaces, which we do not attempt.
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 is the bottleneck.} The oracle ceiling of 0.938 against 0.706 shows that most of the remaining error is anchor localization. Anchors within about 8 px merge and cost about 0.2 F. Coarse-to-fine refinement and a multi-peak Stage 2 are the planned fixes and neither is implemented.
  \item \textbf{No accuracy advantage over classical fitting.} The cascade ties tuned RANSAC at $d=3$ and is slightly behind at $d=5$. Its benefit is differentiable feature-space voting and low accumulator memory. We did not train or evaluate a dense accumulator at $d\ge4$, so the memory ratio is not an accuracy comparison.
  \item \textbf{Seeds and coverage.} The baseline comparison in Table~\ref{tab:main} is one seed. Only the pooling ablation has three seeds. Circle and ellipse have no ablations, and the learned baselines were run for parabolas only. The ablations cover pooling and the number of retained anchors on parabolas only. Probe-set size, noise level, occlusion level and mean pooling were not evaluated.
  \item \textbf{Scale and data.} All main results are at $64\times64$ with 1{,}000 training images and 10 epochs. The benchmark is synthetic, and trends at $128\times128$ are untested. The regression and query baselines are small and briefly trained, so their gap to the geometric methods is specific to this budget.
  \item \textbf{Real images.} The TuSimple results are single runs predate the validation-threshold protocol, use an untuned RANSAC baseline, and handle near-straight and curved lanes with two separate models. Published lane detectors are not evaluated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
A deep Hough transform need not enumerate the full curve parameter space. If a curve family admits a spatial anchor, feature-space voting can be factorized into anchor localization followed by conditional shape voting, which reduces accumulator memory from $O(B^{d})$ to $O(B^{d_1}+kB^{d_2})$ and makes DHT-style voting memory-feasible for families beyond lines. With max pooling over probe shapes, the cascade matches a tuned RANSAC fit on synthetic parabolas and circles, is slightly behind on ellipses, and needs 236\,KB of accumulator memory at $d=5$ where a dense accumulator would need about 4\,GB. Its remaining error sits in Stage 1: Stage 2 reaches F $=0.938$ at the ground-truth anchors, and nearby anchors that merge into one peak are the concrete failure mode. Detection of an unspecified family is outside the scope of this work. Natural next steps are an improved Stage 1, robustness sweeps over noise and occlusion, and a comparison with published lane detectors under their own metric. Code, configurations and the benchmark generator are released with the commands for every table.
